Before You Buy a Hitch Rack for Your E-Bike, Weigh Three Things. Your Bike Is Only One of Them.

The number printed on the rack box is rarely the number that matters. Here's what to check on your car first.

By Tim Street · October 21, 2026 · 7 min read

I spent longer researching a bike rack for my Subaru Outback than I'd like to admit. Not because the choices were confusing, but because the most important numbers weren't on the rack at all. They were in my car's owner's manual and on my bike.

If you're shopping for a rack to carry e-bikes, here's what I wish someone had told me up front.

Number one: what your bikes really weigh

E-bikes are heavy. Cruisers and step-throughs that a lot of riders our age prefer commonly land somewhere in the 50- to 70-pound range, and some go past that. Two of them can put well over 100 pounds on the back of your car before you add the rack itself.

Weigh your actual bike. A bathroom scale works: weigh yourself, then weigh yourself holding the bike, and subtract. Then take the battery off and weigh it again. Most racks are rated per bike, and pulling the battery is one of the easiest ways to get back under a limit. It also keeps the battery out of the Florida sun and out of the way of road grime.

Number two: what your car can actually hold

This is the number most people miss. Your hitch receiver has a size (usually 1¼ or 2 inches) and a rating. Your car also has a tongue weight limit: how much weight it's designed to carry pressing down on the hitch. That limit lives in your owner's manual or on the hitch's label, and it's often lower than people expect on crossovers and wagons.

That tongue weight has to cover everything: both bikes and the rack. A sturdy two-bike platform rack can weigh a good chunk of that limit all by itself. Do the math before you buy, not after.

Number three: what you can comfortably lift

Nobody likes to talk about this one, but it decides whether you'll actually use the rack. Lifting a 60-pound bike up to a tray at bumper height every time you ride gets old fast, and it's a good way to hurt your back.

Look for racks that come with a loading ramp or offer one as an add-on. A ramp lets you roll the bike up instead of lifting it. For a lot of riders, that's the difference between riding three times a week and letting the bike sit in the garage.

Style: why platform racks win for e-bikes

Hanging racks, the kind that hold a bike by its top tube, generally aren't built for e-bike weight, and many step-through frames don't have a top tube to hang from anyway. Platform racks, which hold the bike by its wheels in trays, are the standard choice for e-bikes.

When you compare platform racks, check:

  • Per-bike weight limit, not just the total.
  • Wheelbase and tire width. Long cruisers and fat-tire bikes may not fit standard trays.
  • How it clamps the bike. Frame arms can scratch paint or interfere with a battery; wheel clamps avoid touching the frame.
  • Tilt-away feature, so you can open the hatch without unloading.
  • Locks for both the bikes and the rack itself.

Two Florida-specific details

First, your license plate needs to stay readable. A loaded rack can block it, so look at your setup from behind before you drive off, and consider a plate relocation bracket or lighting kit if the bikes cover it.

Second, heat. Lithium batteries don't like sitting in a closed car on a summer afternoon any more than you do. Take them off the bike for the drive when you can, and don't leave them baking in the car while you're at lunch.

What about trikes?

Electric trikes are a different problem altogether, and most bike racks won't take them. We'll cover trike hauling, including trailers and fold-flat models, in its own guide.

Before you buy: write down three numbers on one piece of paper: your car's tongue weight limit, your rack's weight, and your bikes' weight with the batteries off. If the last two don't fit comfortably inside the first, keep shopping.
