The Pinellas Trail: A Town-to-Town Ride, Not a Long Empty Corridor

Florida's most urban rail-trail, from St. Petersburg to Tarpon Springs, where lunch, water and a place to sit are usually a town away, not miles away.

By Tim Street · October 25, 2026 · 5 min read

Trail at a glance

Length
37.4 miles, linear main trail (Florida Hikes); about 75 miles counting the wider Pinellas Trail Loop network and connections
Surface
Paved, mostly asphalt with some concrete; 12 to 15 feet wide depending on section
County
Pinellas
South end
Demens Landing Park, St. Petersburg
North end
US 19 underpass, Tarpon Springs
Good for
Town-to-town rides; out-and-back rides from a single town; riders who want frequent stops rather than long empty miles

The Pinellas Trail is the opposite of the Suncoast in one important way: lunch, water and a place to sit are usually a town away, not miles away. The trade-off is that you will stop, cross streets and share the path. This is not a place for uninterrupted steady miles.

That makes it a good fit for the way this site rides: ride a little, stop a lot.

What you're actually riding

The Fred Marquis Pinellas Trail follows a former railroad corridor through Pinellas County, roughly in a "C" shape from downtown St. Petersburg northwest to Tarpon Springs. It is flat, paved and, in most places, wide enough for bikes in both directions plus walkers.

South of Clearwater, the corridor is very urban. It is not until you are north of Clearwater that the corridor gains a visual buffer of green space around it. That change in feel matters more than the mileage: the same trail can feel like a city path in the morning and a park ride by afternoon, depending on where you start.

Sections at a glance

These mileposts, from Florida Hikes, help plan a ride without riding the whole trail at once:

  • 0.0 — Demens Landing Park, St. Petersburg
  • 18.2 — Taylor Park, Largo
  • 22.2 — Cleveland Street, Clearwater
  • 25.7 — Main Street, Dunedin
  • 33.0 — Wall Springs Park, Palm Harbor
  • 35.4 — Tarpon Avenue, Tarpon Springs
  • 37.4 — US 19 underpass, Tarpon Springs

A Dunedin-to-Tarpon Springs ride, for example, is roughly 10 miles one way by those mileposts, not a full-trail expedition. That is the scale this overview is built for, and the scale the section guides will cover in detail.

The trade-off nobody puts in the brochure

Because the trail runs through towns, you get frequent crossings, overpasses in the south, and crowds near Dunedin in tourist season. Rider reviews repeatedly mention both sides of that: plenty of places to stop, and plenty of reasons to slow down.

If you want a long, steady training ride, this is not the best choice. If you want a ride where you can stop for lunch, a market, a festival or a beach detour and still be home for dinner, it is hard to beat.

Parking and trailheads

Florida Hikes lists park trailheads including Progress Energy Park and Azalea Park in St. Petersburg, Seminole City Park, Taylor Park in Largo, Downtown Dunedin's Main Street lot, Weaver Park and Hammock Park in Dunedin, Wall Springs Park in Palm Harbor, and North Safford Park in Tarpon Springs.

Treat that as a starting list, not a promise about fees, lot size or restroom hours. Those details change, and they will be checked section by section.

Where to stop

Here is the honest version for an overview: we are not naming specific lunch stops here yet. The section guides for Dunedin and Tarpon Springs will do that work, with the exact exit, how far off the trail it is, whether there is somewhere to lock up a heavy bike, and whether you can see your bike from your table.

Until then, plan on town stops, not trailside stops, and carry water for the stretches between towns.

Tips for e-bike riders

  • Keep it under 20 mph on the trail. That is the trail limit cited by Dunedin and the Pinellas County Sheriff's Office.
  • Check the legal e-bike basics: 750-watt motor max, working pedals required, and a helmet for riders under 16.
  • Stay right except to pass, and announce yourself before passing walkers. This trail is shared, and in town sections it is busy.
  • Plan for overpasses in the south. They are short, but they are real work on a heavy bike or trike.
